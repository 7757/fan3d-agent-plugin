# Motion and pacing

Read this reference when a product introduction needs a shot sequence, camera
language, timing, cuts, a loop, or pacing advice. Discover current camera-motion
choices from the connected Fan3D catalog; do not rely on remembered preset IDs,
counts, or durations.

## Give every move a communication job

Choose motion from the viewer's task:

| Communication job | Useful motion direction |
| --- | --- |
| Reveal the product | Enter or turn toward a composed hero view, then hold long enough to read it |
| Inspect form or finish | Use a restrained pan, turn, or custom endpoint change that reveals one surface relationship at a time |
| Prove a feature | Settle into a stable, readable screen view during the proof; move before or after it rather than continuously competing with it |
| Present an ecosystem | Arrange the devices first, then use camera travel to reveal hierarchy and relationships |
| Add launch energy | Use a small number of decisive camera clips and intentional hard cuts, with a clear resting frame between ideas when needed |
| Create a calm ambient loop | Favor gentle reversible motion and compatible opening and closing camera states |

Select an installed motion using its live human name, description, motion
duration, and insertion duration. Preview it in the actual scene before calling
it suitable; the same move can feel very different with another canvas, device
scale, or layout.

## Design the timeline around beats

Plan meaningful moments before exact timestamps:

- an opening frame that establishes context;
- an arrival or reveal;
- a proof interval where the important screen or product state is easy to
  inspect;
- an optional second angle or ecosystem view;
- a final hold that resolves the movement and supports the claim or CTA.

Convert those moments into explicit project times after inspecting the current
duration and existing clips. Keep every clip inside the project timeline and
avoid overlaps. Leave real timeline space before the first move or after the
last move when a readable hold is part of the concept.

Do not add movement merely to fill the duration. A short stable interval can be
more persuasive than another camera move when the viewer needs to understand a
screen, label, or product relationship.

## Shape camera motion

- Use camera position and orbit for viewpoint, field of view for spatial
  emphasis, and focus treatment for depth. Preserve unrelated inspected camera
  settings when changing an endpoint.
- Prefer one dominant spatial change per clip. A large orbit, large distance
  change, field-of-view change, and focus shift at the same time usually hides
  which idea matters.
- Use ease-out behavior for an arrival that should settle, ease-in-out for a
  controlled showcase, and linear timing only when a deliberately mechanical
  feel supports the product. Use overshooting or back-style timing sparingly
  for playful work.
- Use Jump or Jump Cut only for an intentional edit between composed views.
  It is a hard cut, not a dissolve or a general transition system.
- Keep motion blur restrained during proof intervals and inspect the midpoint
  of any fast move for clipping, unreadable screens, or an unintended back view.

Camera clips can create a multi-shot sequence within one persistent scene. They
do not provide independent scene states: the background, environment, device
set, screen binding, and static copy do not become different merely because the
camera cuts.

## Coordinate product media and sound

Device-screen and moving-background videos begin at project time zero. Source
trimming changes the source range but cannot schedule a later start. If a
feature must appear at an exact beat, the supplied media must already be edited
for that project timing.

Fan3D can use one project background-music track, while a device-screen video's
original audio remains a separate source. Current public controls do not supply
music audition, beat detection, fades, ducking, narration, sound effects, or a
multi-track mix. Therefore:

- align clip boundaries to beat timestamps only when the user or supplied
  production brief provides those timestamps;
- choose bundled music from live text metadata without claiming to have heard
  it;
- treat narration, sound design, detailed mixing, and automatic beat sync as
  supplied-media or external-finishing requirements.

## Respect authoring limits

Camera movement is the primary motion that can be authored reliably through
the current public workflow. Do not promise:

- a new device transform animation, such as a device flying, spinning, or
  entering independently of the camera;
- a new articulation animation when the project does not already contain a
  compatible articulation clip;
- timed screen swaps, timed label-copy changes, or animated background and
  environment states;
- crossfades, masks, layered transitions, or automatic stitching of separately
  rendered projects.

Existing authored animation in a project or template may still render. Inspect
it and preserve it unless the current public controls expose the exact requested
edit. Never substitute direct project-package editing or UI automation for a
missing authoring capability.

## Review pacing truthfully

Render representative frames at the opening, each clip midpoint, immediately
around intentional cuts, the proof moment, and the ending. Use project state and
clip metadata to evaluate timing continuity.

Still preview frames cannot prove full-motion smoothness, audio quality, or
beat synchronization. For a proposed loop, match the authored opening and
closing camera states and compare both frames, but describe seamless playback
as requiring final rendered and human review rather than as guaranteed.
