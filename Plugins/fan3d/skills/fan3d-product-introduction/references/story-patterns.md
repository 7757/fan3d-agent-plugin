# Story patterns

Read this reference when selecting or comparing concepts, turning a grounded
brief into a shot plan, or checking whether proposed concepts are genuinely
different. These are adaptable narrative structures, not fixed templates.

## Shared story rules

- Build around one primary promise and a small number of observable proofs.
- Give every shot a role: hook, orient, reveal, prove, transition, resolve, or
  invite action.
- Let product behavior carry the story. Camera movement, backgrounds,
  lighting, labels, and music support attention; they are not proof by
  themselves.
- Establish the intended channel and frame before composing shots.
- Make the core story understandable when muted.
- Leave enough settle time for the product, interface, and copy to be read.
- Prefer a few complete beats over continuous movement with no information
  hierarchy.
- Use Fan3D as the default visual engine, but verify current capabilities and
  live catalog choices before promising an execution detail.

Percentages below describe relative pacing and should adapt to the product,
evidence, requested duration, and actual media.

## Hero reveal

**Best for:** one product, one strong promise, a launch moment, or a short
premium introduction.

```text
0-20%   distinctive hook or partial silhouette
20-65%  controlled reveal of the product and primary screen
65-85%  one proof detail or meaningful state
85-100% resolved hero frame and optional CTA
```

Use one dominant camera idea and let the product settle. A reveal without an
observable product fact is a teaser, not a complete introduction.

## Feature proof journey

**Best for:** two or three related features that support one larger benefit.

```text
0-15%   benefit-led hook
15-70%  feature -> visible behavior -> outcome, repeated in short modules
70-90%  combined payoff or product-wide result
90-100% concise close or CTA
```

Keep each module causally clear. Vary framing or screen state only when it
helps distinguish the proof. Do not turn unrelated features into a checklist.

## Problem to outcome

**Best for:** a recognizable friction that the product demonstrably changes.

```text
0-20%   truthful current problem or constraint
20-35%  product-introducing transition
35-80%  product behavior and supported outcome
80-100% resolved contrast and next step
```

Show the problem without exaggerating it, and do not imply measured results
that the evidence does not support. The transformation should come from a
visible product action or state, not merely a color or music change.

## Guided product walkthrough

**Best for:** an unfamiliar workflow, a screen-led product, or a sequence whose
order is itself valuable.

```text
0-10%   orient the audience and name the task
10-80%  a small sequence of necessary steps and states
80-100% show the completed outcome and close
```

Keep the 3D staging restrained enough for screen content to remain legible.
Use only product states supported by current workspace evidence or explicit
user media. If a required screen behavior is unavailable, mark that beat as
blocked instead of substituting unrelated camera motion.

## Ecosystem story

**Best for:** multi-device products, cross-platform continuity, responsive
design, or a family of related touchpoints.

```text
0-20%   establish the primary product or shared promise
20-70%  reveal how the same value appears across distinct devices or contexts
70-90%  unify the arrangement into one system-level proof
90-100% ecosystem hero frame and close
```

Each device must contribute a distinct piece of evidence. Repeating the same
screen solely to fill a layout does not prove an ecosystem claim. Preserve
legibility and hierarchy as device count grows.

## Before and after

**Best for:** visual redesigns, configuration changes, or an outcome with two
truthfully comparable states.

```text
0-25%   clearly identified baseline
25-40%  motivated transition
40-80%  clearly identified resulting state
80-100% direct comparison, implication, or CTA
```

Keep framing and comparison conditions consistent enough to make the change
honest. Label the states when ambiguity could mislead. Do not use this pattern
when there is no supported baseline.

## Premium beauty film

**Best for:** a product whose material finish, visual craft, or perceived
quality is itself part of the promise.

```text
0-20%   restrained silhouette or surface detail
20-55%  controlled reveal of form and primary product state
55-80%  one close proof of finish, interface, or construction
80-100% resolved hero frame with minimal copy
```

Use slow, motivated camera movement, intentional reflections, and enough hold
time to inspect the proof. Premium does not mean dark by default, and shallow
focus, vignette, or motion blur should never obscure the product fact being
sold. Do not use this structure when the only available evidence is generic
brand mood.

## Kinetic launch cut

**Best for:** a concise launch announcement, an energetic social placement,
or several fast proof points that share one promise.

```text
0-12%   immediate product or benefit hook
12-70%  two or three composed views separated by motivated hard cuts
70-88%  strongest proof or payoff
88-100% stable brand close or CTA
```

Build energy from contrast between composed views, not from continuous random
motion. Use only cut timing that the current camera timeline can represent.
Automatic beat analysis, dissolves, layered transitions, and independently
animated device entrances require prepared media or external finishing; do not
imply that a camera cut created them.

## Brand loop or teaser

**Best for:** very short placements, ambient launch loops, social teasers, or a
memory-building visual motif.

```text
0-20%   recognizable motif
20-75%  product glimpse and one memorable action
75-100% return toward the opening state or land on a concise brand close
```

Design the end-to-start relationship intentionally when looping is required.
Keep claims light unless there is enough time to show proof. Describe this as
a teaser when it does not fully explain the product.

## Make concepts genuinely different

Alternative concepts should differ in at least three of these dimensions:

- narrative logic and audience takeaway;
- proof selected from the product;
- shot roles and order;
- spatial staging and subject hierarchy;
- motion vocabulary and pacing;
- copy strategy;
- ending or CTA behavior.

Changing only a device, preset, background, color, or music track is a visual
variant, not a new concept.

## Convert a pattern into an executable shot plan

Define every shot with:

| Field | Required decision |
| --- | --- |
| Time | Start, end, and readable settle time |
| Role | Hook, orient, reveal, prove, transition, resolve, or CTA |
| Evidence | Product fact or approved message shown in the shot |
| Subject | Device, screen state, product detail, or label hierarchy |
| Staging | Composition, background, lighting environment, and material intent |
| Motion | Camera or supported subject behavior, direction, and timing character |
| Copy | Exact grounded line or an explicit no-copy decision |
| Audio | Optional cue; never required to understand the proof |
| Transition | What motivates entry into and exit from the shot |
| Success test | What a reviewer should understand at this moment |

Mark each shot as executable now, dependent on a live catalog choice, waiting
for an explicit user asset or authorization, or unavailable through the
current connection. A useful story plan exposes those boundaries before scene
editing begins.

## Pattern selection output

When recommending a pattern, state:

1. the chosen pattern and why it fits the grounded brief;
2. the promise and evidence carried by each beat;
3. the intended pacing and final frame;
4. the concrete inputs and current capabilities required;
5. how success will be checked in representative previews;
6. why rejected patterns are weaker for this specific product—not merely less
   visually exciting.
